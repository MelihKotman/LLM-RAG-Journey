English is a language with comparatively little inflectional morphology: a single root word rarely changes shape to express grammatical meaning. Instead of attaching several suffixes to a root, English tends to use separate function words: our houses, from our houses, the ones in our houses. A word like house has only a handful of surface forms: house, houses, house's, houses'. Because of this, the vocabulary of common English word forms stays relatively small and stable, and most tokenizers can cover frequent words with whole-word tokens plus a modest set of subword pieces for rare or novel words. This is very different from agglutinative languages like Turkish, where a single root can combine with dozens of suffixes to produce hundreds of distinct surface forms, each carrying its own grammatical meaning such as plurality, possession, case, or question marking. When a byte pair encoding tokenizer is trained on English text versus Turkish text with the same number of merge operations, the two corpora typically compress differently: English needs comparatively fewer merges to reach whole-word tokens, while Turkish keeps producing new merges as it discovers root-suffix boundaries. Comparing the two directly, on matched text, makes this difference visible and measurable rather than just anecdotal. Students, teachers, and researchers who study this often build small experiments like this one to see the effect for themselves.
